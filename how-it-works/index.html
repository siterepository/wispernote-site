What happens between the key and the cursor.

Four steps, about a second. Here is all of it.

The four steps.

Each step names what leaves your device at the moment it leaves, and who receives it.

  1. 1

    Step 1 of 4: Capture

    When you press and hold the key, Wisper Note opens the microphone and starts recording, and the pill appears near your cursor so you can see that it is listening. Audio lives in memory. Nothing is written to disk at any point, in any mode.

    What leaves your deviceNothing. The audio has not left the machine yet.

  2. 2

    Step 2 of 4: Transcribe

    The audio streams to our transcription service while you are still speaking, so the transcript is finished about as soon as you are. The words in your personal dictionary go with it as hints, which is why names come back spelled the way you spell them.

    What leaves your deviceYour audio, and your dictionary terms as transcription hints — to our speech-to-text service.

  3. 3

    Step 3 of 4: Clean up

    The finished transcript goes to our cleanup service with your tone setting and the name of the app you are typing into. That step removes fillers, applies your self-corrections, punctuates, breaks paragraphs, normalises numbers and dates, and expands any snippet you triggered.

    What leaves your deviceThe transcript, your tone setting, and the identifier of the app you are typing into — its bundle id or process name, not its contents — to our text-processing service.

  4. 4

    Step 4 of 4: Insert

    Wisper Note types the result at your cursor. It pastes by default, falls back to typing character by character, and — if the app refuses both, which password fields and administrator windows do — it puts the text on your clipboard and tells you to press ⌘V. That third case is a state with a message, not a silent failure.

    What leaves your deviceNothing, unless you keep a history — in which case the finished text is stored with our database provider. Turn history off and this step stores nothing at all.

What we send, and what we do not.

These two lists are generated from the same file the desktop app’s Privacy pane reads, so the website and the product cannot drift apart.

Sent

  • Your audio, only while the dictation key is held
  • The resulting transcript
  • The identifier of the app you are typing into — its bundle id or process name, not its contents
  • Your dictionary terms, sent as transcription hints

Never sent

  • Anything you did not dictate
  • The contents of the app you are typing into
  • Your keystrokes
  • Your screen
  • Audio written to disk — it is held in memory and zeroed on stop, in every mode

Privacy mode stops us saving anything. It does not stop your audio leaving your device — transcription still happens on our servers. There is no offline mode in this version. The full privacy policy names every company that receives anything and links to each of their policies.

How we measure whether it made anything up.

We do not publish an accuracy percentage.

Every dictation product in this category quotes one, and none of the ones we read publish the audio, the method, the sample size or the date behind it. A number you cannot reproduce is not evidence, so we are not going to add another one.

What we publish instead is the test. The polish-core package specifies a fixed set of 60 utterances — chat, email, documents and code, across five tone levels and six languages, with self-corrections, numbers, snippet triggers and dictionary terms deliberately seeded into them. Every build runs the cleanup prompt over that set and checks two things:

  • Does it still mean what you said?

    A semantic match against the expected output. The bar is 90%.

  • Did it add anything?

    Any run of three or more words in the output that cannot be traced to the input or to a snippet is a failure. The bar for that one is zero. Not “low” — zero. A build that invents words does not ship.

Current state

There is no number on this page yet, because nothing has been measured yet. The golden set and the harness are specified in the polish-core package and its source says so in as many words: they land in Sprint 1, and they have not been run against a shipping build.

When the harness runs, the semantic-match rate and the added-content count appear in this block — read from the report file at build time, with the date and the short commit hash they ran on. Until then the method stands on its own, which is the point of publishing a method rather than a figure.

When it gets it wrong.

It will. These are the four ways it happens most, and what the product does about each of them.

A word it has never heard

Add it to your dictionary. The term goes to the transcriber as a hint the next time you speak, so the word comes back right instead of being repaired after the fact — and it is applied again as a correction on the finished text, which catches the ones the hint did not reach.

Your dictionary →

A name spelled two ways

The dictionary has a sounds-like field for exactly this. One entry carries the spelling you want and the way the name is actually said, so both pronunciations resolve to the one spelling rather than to whichever the transcriber preferred that day.

Your dictionary →

A room too loud

Wisper Note shows you that it has no usable signal rather than transcribing the room, and nothing is inserted. Whisper mode is the wrong tool here: it raises the gain, which raises the room along with your voice. It is for a quiet voice, not a loud room.

Whisper mode →

No connection

Nothing is queued and nothing is captured. There is no draft waiting to be sent when the network comes back, because the audio was never held anywhere to send. The pill says it cannot reach the network rather than recording something it has no way to finish.
